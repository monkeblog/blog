I've been seeing some discourse about the Epstein interview, with some people being impressed by his familiarity with science while others saw him as a bloviating circumlocuter. Despite this, I didn't see much discussion about Epstein's actual correctness, which I will do here.

To be upfront, I think Epstein was a giant pseud. That is not to say he was unintelligent (that is another discussion), but when it comes to science all of his knowledge was clearly second or third hand and he had no real understanding of what he was talking about. Which is about what I would expect for a socialite rubbing elbows with scientists hoping that some of the intellectual veneer will wear off on him. This means that Epstein was directionally correct in some avant-garde ways that could impress a normie, but he was unable to elaborate on them or provide good examples.

I think it is harder for people to pick up on this because they are unfamiliar with the scientific perspective he was espousing. Broadly, his perspective is informed by complex systems science, which was spearheaded by the Santa Fe Institute. Complex systems grew as a field because people realized that many systems we cared about understanding/predicting were resistant to the Newtonian approach of setting initial conditions, boundary conditions, and dynamics, and then simulating the system propagating deterministically through time. Once a system accommodates some amount of degrees of freedom or nonlinearity, it can become unpredictable -- as in, no amount of precision in measuring initial conditions will lead to a bounded prediction of the system dynamics. These are called chaotic systems. The three-body problem or the double pendulum is the simplest and most famous of chaotic systems. 
Complex systems are another level of system that are resistant to Newtonian modeling, and can include the aforementioned chaotic dynamics as well as interacting units, feedback loops, emergent behaviors, reflexivity, randomness, etc. that also make them hard or impossible to predict. These systems are common and describe almost any system you would care about at the human level.

Epstein uses the existence of these systems (among other tools in the anti-mechanism repertoire, like problems in measurement theory, the imperfect correspondence between words and referents, and the general failure of formal systems to describe everything) to make blanket statements about how natural phenomena are difficult to predict. This is true. The systems he discusses, the financial system and the human body, are indeed complex and difficult to predict. But his eagerness to dismiss any sort of knowledge betrays his desire to play the aloof intellectual rather than having any curiosity about the system in question. It boggles the mind that he claims to not know a single person who understands finance better than him, but then defaults to a "what does understand mean? Did you know that systems are complex so we can't know anything about them? Did you know that anytime you measure something you're making a mistake?" when lightly pressed for details on anything. It is still possible to understand complex systems (to varying degrees)! The complex systems claim is that we just can't know them in a totalizing way, as in we cannot have perfect knowledge of everything that will ever happen in the system. The fact that the human body is a complex system does not mean I cannot predict what is going to happen when someone falls into an incinerator.

I have unfortunately seen the archetype where someone will use the (incredibly fascinating) facts about complex systems as a shield to cover up their own lack of knowledge, or as a criticism of any person trying to understand complex systems in a classical way, or as criticism of mainstream science as a whole. So it was easy for me to spot it in Epstein here. It is indeed very tempting to fall into the hole of thinking you have found hidden knowledge, and then using it to feel superior. But in this case it is a little like teaching a 5th grader that Gödel's incompleteness theorems exist, and then claiming that 5th grader knows more math than Gauss.

So that is the broad thrust of my critique. By itself it's not too bad, and is a perspective one can fall into just by being a little too excited about some of the interesting ideas running around in science today. But what really nails Epstein's distilled pseudness for me is the amount of blatant errors he makes in the interview. Not an exhaustive list:

- Epstein uses 1/0 as an example of an indeterminate expression that could be solved once Newtonian calculus was invented. In the "calculus equivalent" case the expression 1/n as n approaches 0 diverges and calculus doesn't really help us with finding a value for this problem. Strange thing to say for someone who claims to be a mathematician.
- Epstein claims that no one knows what quark flavors charm and strange are. In reality we understand quark flavors about as well as we understand that different quantum particles, characterized by differing quantum numbers, exist. In the steelman case, maybe he is referring to the mystery of why the standard model is the way it is but 1) this is not attributable to complex system shenanigans and 2) this is strange as an example of a "physics mystery" when lower hanging fruit is available, like dark matter or dark energy or the gravity unification problem.
- Epstein says that light goes at different speeds (implied: in the same medium). I guess he's never heard of Jüdische Physik!  
- Epstein describes compressing music as "cutting music into pieces and squishing the bits together". He then claims he is more interested in the pieces that are taken out than the pieces that remain. I don't think he knows that the pieces that are taken out are redundancies, generally. He is directionally correct in that there is something extremely interesting happening phenomenologically wrt music but it has nothing to do with the bits that are compressed out, as evidenced by the fact that the music mostly sounds the same. 
- Epstein claims that Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle didn't write anything. This is simply false (save Socrates) and has been rightly memed on. I think it is possible Epstein perhaps overheard someone discussing a famous section in Phaedrus where Socrates critiques writing, and then didn't remember the details. Regardless, anyone with a passing familiarity with philosophy would know that Plato and Aristotle wrote stuff.
- Epstein claims that the word quantum in quantum physics is because quantum physics studies things that are very small, at the subatomic level, and quantum means small, hence the name. This is not true. The name came from the experimental results showing that electron energy levels only came in discrete packets and were therefore "quantized" hence "quantum".
- Epstein claims that we don't understand why bananas ripen faster when placed next to another fruit. It's kind of funny that out of all the things we actually don't understand about biology, Epstein chose an extremely well understood pathway. Once one fruit starts to release ethylene, then that ethylene will diffuse into other fruits and in a positive feedback loop those fruits will start producing more ethylene. Ethylene triggers a bunch of other processes associated with ripening, like chlorophyll breakdown, starch breakdown, etc.
- Epstein's accounting of the history of science is really weird. He claims the alchemists are the ones with the machine perspective, and says alchemists believed that they could change the numbers of protons/neutrons on a metal to transmute it into gold. First, alchemists were working entirely in the chemical realm, and never tried changing the number of protons in a metal nor did they have an understanding of protons characterizing elemental metals. Second, this is a scenario where the machine perspective of nature works great! It's true that elemental metals are almost completely characterized by proton/neutron count, and it's true you can transmute one metal into another by changing its proton count.

There is also a lot of stuff that are not blatant errors, but things I wanted to discuss anyways:

- Epstein tries to say that mathematics only works well when modelling reality because our mind created mathematics. By itself this is a pretty interesting idea that echoes Kant. And maybe someone told him about Wigner's "Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics" essay. He then uses an example where a species which only senses radio waves would not perceive a table as solid like we do. This is true but does not support his point, as the radio-wave sensing species would still be able to describe radio waves mathematically. I understand his intention in that he was trying to make an argument about the Umwelt of an organism determining reality for that organism, but his example simply doesn't support his point.
- Epstein adopts an anti-Newtonian anti-machine anti-measurement anti-formalism perspective, yet somehow fails to mention the failure of Hilbert's program, Wittgenstein, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, or any Continental philosopher who has this topic as their main thing.
- Epstein mentions Stuart Kauffman's work on "genetic algorithms" and saying that it was a way to predict the unpredictable and make money (?). By this I assume he is talking about Kauffman's work on gene regulatory networks or on fitness landscapes, which maybe can be called genetic algorithms back then? But usually genetic algorithms refer to an optimization technique that is analogous to natural selection. And I do know that people at the Santa Fe Institute were interested in applying complex systems science to finance but I don't see how Kauffman's work on genetics could be directly involved in that. Possibly Epstein has insider info that I don't here.
- After talking about alchemists, Epstein said that Newton proved them wrong by postulating that nothing could move without a force, and then discovering gravity as a mysterious force that explained unmoved moving. And then he says that scientists took all this stuff to understand chaos and complexity. This is "not even wrong" territory. There is nothing post-machine about the Newtonian physics worldview (much the opposite), and also Newton himself did not prove alchemists wrong, being an alchemist himself. It is additionally true that scientists used the limitations of Newtonian physics to discover things about chaos and complexity but Epstein elides a lot and doesn't justify a full jump to anti-mechanism.
- Epstein cites Schrödinger's short book What is Life?  as an example of someone failing to formalize the difference between life and non-life. Since the notion of the aperiodic crystal in the book was an explicit inspiration to the discovery of DNA, and the other considerations Schrödinger mentions esp wrt negentropy are more or less correct, I wouldn't characterize it is a failure. It is entirely true however that it has failed to COMPLETELY formalize life. If Schrödinger were alive today I think he would disagree with Epstein's characterization, though Schrödinger and Epstein would have likely gotten along for reasons other than scientific accuracy! 🥸
- "The soul is the dark matter of the brain" is the most pseud shit i've ever heard in my entire life.
- Epstein's relating of Leibniz's argument that perception and consciousness cannot arise from mechanism is actually ok.
- "Women having hidden intuitive knowledge" and "children should be learning how to feel, not learn algebra" and "science and math are old-fashioned" is clearly Epstein cosplaying what he thinks of as a wise heterodox public intellectual. lol. There is no way he actually believes this stuff beyond mere words.

A broad pattern throughout is that Epstein strings together a series of points that are in the neighborhood of valid and interesting work, but the connection between points is completely incoherent. His examples repeatedly betray a lack of understanding of the point he is making. I could continue cataloguing errors but I am falling down the rabbit hole of Epstein's fractal incorrectness and it is just turning into nitpicking at this point.
In short, Epstein makes such devastating errors in his casual speech that he cannot reasonably claim to be any sort of mathematician, scientist, physicist, or philosopher. The interesting topics that he does bring up seem lifted from socializing with academics or reading pop science, with no first order knowledge or thinking of his own. He is firmly a dilettante, which is fine and something many are, including myself, but his pretending to be something more is bamboozling people even after his death.
