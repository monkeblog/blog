This is a corrollary to Tanner Greer's ["the making of a techno-nationalist elite"](https://americanaffairsjournal.org/2025/11/the-making-of-a-techno-nationalist-elite/) and ["35 theses on the WASPs."](https://scholars-stage.org/35-theses-on-the-wasps/) 

In these back to back essays he details the rise of the techno-nationalist elite class of Americans that led the Gilded Age after the Civil War, eventually becoming known as the Eastern Establishment, and the fall of this Eastern Establishment throughout the Great Depression and the 20th century. I highly recommend reading both, as they provided a fascinating historical context for the industrial bedrock of the United States that we live in.

All of this is a critical review of the book "The Technological Republic" by Alex Karp [^1] and Nicholas Zamiska, which, in his eyes, makes a weak argument to have the new Silicon Valley elites form a new techno-nationalist elite governing class of Americans akin to the Eastern Establishment of the Gilded Age. He uses a framework of what elements a governing class needs to sustain its interests, which he aptly describes here:

> Any governing class requires three things: a political coalition to which it owes allegiance and over which it exercises influence; an economic base that provides this class with wealth and unites its members around shared material interests; and finally, a set of institutions, rituals, and social customs that give this class a culture distinct from the country at large. Absent the first two, a leadership class lacks the power to lead; absent the latter two, it lacks the ability to act as a class.

A big point T. Greer makes is that the Silicon Valley elites are, unlike the Civil War generation, missing an established way of raising their next generation of members. The Civil War generation clearly established institutions and customs that allowed their children to fill their shoes as they came of age, through boarding schools, Ivy Leagues, intermarriage, and so on.

I just have a few thoughts I'd like to contribute on this topic.

Unfortunately, it's true. It's clear the Silicon Valley elite are not thinking seriously about building the right foundations for their children. One only needs to take a glance at the familial arrangements (or lack thereof) of some of these elites and the discourse on how hard it is to date in SF. Alex Karp himself said the idea of children gives him "hives." [^2] However, where they cannot propagate their genes, they can propagate their memes [^3]. In this way, perhaps there is a developing system of institutions in place to raise the next generation of engineering elites, just not biologically. 

> That this world, and all its people, are diseased. Free will is a myth. Religion is a joke. We are all connected by something far greater. **Memes**. The DNA of the soul. They shape our will. They are the culture -- they are everything we pass on.
    > - Monsoon, Metal Gear Rising: Revengeance

This Silicon Valley engineering elite class is distinct from the Eastern Establishment in that while it has a distinct set of institutions, rituals, and social customs, they are not exclusive by any means in other ways. While the Eastern Establishment elites were mostly relegated to WASPs at the time and other ethnic groups were excluded from power, the Silicon Valley elites are willing to invite anyone to join them so long as they adopt their cultural ways and beliefs. It's sustaining a population through immigration rather than reproduction. You might say that in the long run it's unsustainable, and it may have been for the Gilded Age if they tried such a thing, but in the 21st century global society, the Silicon Valley elites are able to pull intelligent young people from almost anywhere in the world to join them, which is a hugely vast pool of people. The doors are open in American universities for the elites of international countries and city suburbs to send their children to, a decent amount of which will study engineering. The Silicon Valley elites fought hard to protect H1B visas, which shows their need for political power to sustain this process. Yes, it is internally unsustainable, but this system can definitely stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent.

---

The enlistment of Silicon Valley elites rather than reproduction is also necessary because the generational cycle is much shorter than that of the Eastern Establishment. The startup software industry, the economic base of the Silicon Valley elites, from which their power stems, rests crucially on engineering brilliance and energy. It's the source of all the innovations that expanded the industry which serves to continually amass more wealth. This resource doesn't come without its constraints, however. Intelligence and energy comes most easily to youth, especially energy. Sure, someone like Jensen Huang is in his 60s, and many of the Silicon Valley elites are well into their 30s now, but the forefront of innovation needs new young startup founders almost every few years or so dedicating all of their waking thoughts to their work. The demand for the speed of innovation is even faster, and the elites just can't afford to wait on their children coming of age to be the next startup founders. On top of that, there's no guarantee that a techbro's children will be just as brilliant, driven, or energetic as they are. You aren't seeing Bill Gates' or Steve Jobs' children founding new startups. Moreover, if a system were to be created that purposefully prepared children to be the next startup founders, it would go against the ideas of liberalism prevalent among many of the California elites.

---

T. Greer mentions that a new development of the Gilded Age was that of the businessman, a new profession in the newly established corporation as an economic institution. To accomodate this profession, the schools to serve the Eastern Establishment, the Ivy Leagues, were shaped to raise students to be these businessmen. 

In like fashion, you could say that the developments of the 20th century have led to the formation of a new profession, namely the white-collar (software) engineer. Previously, engineering was not seen as an elite profession, but belonging to the workers and not the intellectuals. But as World War II cemented the power of scientific brilliance and the military-industrial complex in the ensuing Cold War, it turned out that engineering was pretty hard and innovations required more scientific intelligence, which meant more schooling. As a result people were willing to pay big money and salaries to those that could do it, and so more specialized schooling was developed to 1.) find unique talent and 2.) train people for this work. Bigger salaries for engineers led to the creation of the white-collar engineer, living closer to an upper-class life, culminating so far in the software engineer (which is being dramatically changed by LLMs but that's another topic).

It's quite common nowadays for families of means in the upper-middle class suburbs of America to send their children to engineering schools, or for students at elite universities to study engineering, where the elite engineering companies can recruit them to join their ranks. A big salary, along with rising equity and shares, can amass large amounts of wealth that contributes to engineering as a suitably prestigious profession to pursue. Nowadays in universities around America, young people can attend and be prepared to join the engineering corporations as the next elites. In the Gilded Age, the Ivy Leagues shaped curricula to train the businessmen, and we can see that a new set of universities in America are shaping their curricula to become pipelines to work for the Silicon Valley engineering elite companies, with heavy sponsorships from the Silicon Valley corporations. Stanford, Berkeley, Waterloo, Purdue, Georgia Tech, UT Austin, to name some. Notice how none of these are Ivy Leagues. 

With all this in place, why would the Silicon Valley elites bother to think about the foundations for their children? (If there even are any) Their memes are already propagating so much faster than their genes ever could, with tons of youth around the world are aspiring to join them already.

***

I've said all this just to describe a process I see at work in our society today, and has been developing well over the past twenty or so years. I've said basically nothing about whether I like it or not, or whether I think it's good. 

In short, I'm not sure if I like this. I am pretty strongly against any form of elitism. This idea that society is driven by an elite class of citizens implies a separation between people, one in which benefits and advancements come to one at the expense of the masses. It's important to note that in the Gilded Age, economic power may have been excluded from the white ethnics (Irish, Italians, etc.) but women and black people couldn't even vote, so who knows if life was improving for them as well. 

There must be a new way for society to advance intellectually without this structure of elitism. Too long has scientific, intellectual, and engineering development been pursued by an isolated group of minds while keeping everyone else out, unable to benefit and learn. Of course, we need to find talent, recognize that talent, and give it space to do great work, but this is distinct from forming an privileged class of citizens. How can we build a culture that promotes knowledge and accomplishment, but also where new knowledge benefits everyone?
